Watch, allow and ignore lists: rogue device detection

Three rule lists that decide what is authorised on your network, what should page you the moment it appears, and what you never want to hear about again.

01 The three lists #

All three work the same way — a match condition against an endpoint — but they answer different questions and have very different consequences.

Allow list
What is authorised. Anything that matches is authorised; anything that matches nothing becomes a rogue. Inverted logic, and the only list that can generate findings on its own.
Watch list
What is interesting. Matching endpoints raise an event every time they appear on the network. Says nothing about whether they are allowed.
Ignore list
What is noise. Matching endpoints disappear from every UDT view and are never flagged, counted or alerted on.

02 The allow list turns rogue detection on #

“Rogue: 0” usually means “not checked”

With no allow rules configured, every endpoint's authorisation state is unclassified — neither authorised nor rogue — and the rogue counter sits at zero. That is not a clean bill of health. Rogue detection does nothing until you tell UDT what “allowed” means.

Once at least one allow rule exists, the sweeper evaluates every non-ignored endpoint every 60 seconds:

  • Matches any allow rule → authorised.
  • Matches none → rogue, and a rogue detected event is raised recording the switch and port it was on at the time.
  • An operator's manual Flag rogue or authorisation is pinned and is never overwritten by the rules.

Deleting the last allow rule returns everything to unclassified rather than declaring the whole network rogue.

04 Rolling it out safely #

Switching on rogue detection in one step on a mature network produces hundreds of findings and destroys everyone's confidence in the feature. Stage it instead.

Do the ignore list first

Silence what you already know is noise — lab subnets, guest VLANs, randomised MACs, test gear. Every endpoint you ignore now is one you do not have to triage later.

Write broad allow rules before narrow ones

Start with the subnets and vendors that account for the bulk of the estate: the corporate client subnets, the server VLANs, the phone OUI, the printer OUI. Aim to cover 90% with a handful of rules.

Add the rules with alerting still off

Create no alert rule for udt_rogue_endpoint yet. The Watch Lists tab lists rogues on screen, which is all you need for the next step.

Work the list to zero

Everything remaining is either a genuine gap in your allow rules or something that deserves attention. Either extend a rule or investigate the device.

Only then turn on alerting

With a quiet baseline, an alert on udt_rogue_endpoint means something new and genuinely unexpected, and people will act on it.

Prefer subnets and OUIs over MAC lists

An allow list of individual MAC addresses becomes a maintenance burden the day someone replaces a laptop. Rules that describe policy — “anything in the corporate client subnets”, “any Polycom phone” — keep working as hardware turns over.

05 The watch list #

The watch list is about attention, not permission. A matching endpoint raises a watch seen event whenever it holds an active session, de-duplicated to at most one event per endpoint per hour so a device that stays connected does not generate a stream.

What it is genuinely useful for:

  • Devices that should never move. A payment terminal, a controller, a piece of medical equipment — you want to know if it turns up on a different port.
  • Equipment under investigation. A machine suspected of causing a problem, watched until it is resolved.
  • Decommissioned hardware. Kit that was supposed to have left the building; a watch hit means it did not.
  • Contractor or loan devices. Temporary equipment you want a record of.

Individual endpoints can also be watched directly with the Watch button on the endpoint page, without writing a rule.

06 The ignore list #

Ignored endpoints vanish completely: excluded from counters, from search results by default, from rogue evaluation and from every alert. This makes the ignore list powerful and worth using carefully.

Reasonable candidates:

  • The ZenPlus appliance's own interfaces and other management addresses.
  • Lab, test and staging subnets where churn is constant and meaningless.
  • Guest wireless ranges, if guests are not in scope for tracking.
  • Randomised MAC addresses, if privacy addressing makes them noise for you.
An ignore rule is a blind spot

Ignoring a broad subnet to quiet the rogue list also means nothing in that subnet will ever be flagged, watched or counted again. If the goal is only to reduce noise, an allow rule is almost always the better instrument — it keeps the devices visible while marking them expected.

Ignoring an endpoint by hand from its detail page is pinned and never undone by rule changes, in the same way manual authorisation is.

07 Alerting on it #

Under Alerting → Alert Rules, three UDT metrics correspond to these lists:

MetricFires whenGood severity
udt_rogue_endpointAn endpoint stops matching every allow ruleWarning, or Critical on sensitive segments
udt_watch_endpointA watched endpoint appears (max once per hour each)Warning
udt_new_endpointAny MAC is seen for the first timeInfo — noisy on a large network

These are event-driven, so the threshold field acts as an on/off switch rather than a number. Each alert carries the endpoint's MAC, hostname, IP, switch and port, and de-duplicates per endpoint for the rule's cooldown period.

Scope matters more than severity. A rule limited to one device group — the switches serving a secure area, say — produces alerts someone will read. A global udt_new_endpoint rule on a campus network produces an alert every few minutes and will be muted within a week.
